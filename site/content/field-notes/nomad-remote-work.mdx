Travel gives you new places to explore. Programming gives you work that can often be done from almost anywhere. Combining the two sounds simple until your internet drops before a client call, your laptop battery runs low, or a mountain stop turns out to have nowhere comfortable to work.

A reliable programmer-nomad setup is not about carrying every gadget you own. It is about being prepared for the problems that interrupt work while leaving enough energy to enjoy the journey.

This guide covers equipment, connectivity, development workflow, security, and planning habits that make working while traveling more manageable.

## 1. Build a travel-ready development setup

Your laptop is the centre of your mobile workspace. Choose a machine that handles your **actual** workload-not specs that look good in a brochure.

Before a trip, check:

- Battery health and realistic battery life under your normal workload.
- Available storage and free disk space.
- Charger compatibility and a spare charging cable.
- USB-C hub or adapters required for your devices.
- Access to repositories, documentation, and essential tools.
- A working backup and recovery plan.

If you use containers, large monorepos, or local AI models, hardware requirements may be higher than for lightweight editor-only work.

**The goal is a setup that works reliably, not the smallest possible bag.** See [Gear & Tools](/gear) for what I travel with today.

## 2. Treat internet access as a system

Accommodation Wi‑Fi should be your primary connection when it is suitable-not your only plan.

Before booking for a work-heavy trip, ask about:

- Download and upload speeds.
- Stability during video calls.
- Mobile coverage inside the property.
- Power availability and backup arrangements.
- A desk or quiet workspace.

Whenever possible, test the connection before scheduling an important meeting or deployment.

### Create a backup connection

A practical stack:

1. Accommodation Wi‑Fi or Ethernet as primary.
2. Phone hotspot with a suitable mobile data plan.
3. A second network option when the trip justifies it.

<Callout title="India-specific note">
Dual-SIM phones can help when two carriers cover a destination differently-but two SIMs do not guarantee redundancy if coverage is poor in a remote valley. Check coverage for the **actual** destination, not your home city.
</Callout>

For a deeper walkthrough, read [Internet backup plans for traveling programmers](/field-notes/developer-internet-backup).

## 3. Plan for power, not just charging

Pack for your real requirements:

- The correct laptop charger.
- A USB-C power bank if your laptop supports charging from one.
- A spare cable for essential devices.
- A compact multi-port charger where appropriate.
- Offline copies of important travel and work information.

Verify the power bank’s watt-hour rating and output-a phone bank may not charge a laptop properly.

In places with unreliable electricity, identify alternate spots to charge and work. Do not assume Wi‑Fi advertising means dependable power.

## 4. Make your development workflow work offline

Know what you can do without connectivity before you leave reliable networks behind.

Before you go:

- Pull the repositories you expect to work on.
- Commit or safely stash unfinished changes.
- Download required documentation.
- Install and verify dependencies.
- Prepare local environment configuration without exposing secrets.
- Cache container images and build dependencies when practical.
- Note which tasks need VPN, cloud APIs, or remote databases.

Divide your task list:

**Offline:** Code, tests, refactors, documentation that needs no remote services.

**Low bandwidth:** Occasional sync or small downloads.

**Online dependent:** Meetings, deployments, remote environments.

See [How to code without internet while traveling](/field-notes/offline-coding-travel) for a focused workflow guide.

## 5. Protect your devices and work

Travel means less control than a home office. Laptops can be lost, stolen, or left on unfamiliar networks.

Before departure:

- Enable full-disk encryption where supported.
- Keep OS, browser, and dev tools updated.
- Use a password manager and strong MFA.
- Protect SSH keys, API tokens, and cloud credentials.
- Store recovery codes separately from your primary device.
- Maintain encrypted backups and test restore.
- Follow employer or client security policies.

Avoid credentials in source code, travel notes, or unencrypted backups. Use approved VPN or secure access on shared networks when required.

## 6. Design your workday around the destination

Avoid stacking a heavy coding day with long transfers, sightseeing, and critical meetings.

Instead:

- Schedule focused work in your most productive hours.
- Group meetings into predictable windows.
- Use travel days for lighter tasks when you can.
- Download maps, tickets, and essentials before departure.
- Buffer for delays and connectivity gaps.
- Communicate availability and time zones to your team.

A quiet morning for coding and an afternoon exploring often beats trying to work continuously while moving.

## 7. Choose destinations with work requirements in mind

A destination can be wonderful for travel without fitting every kind of remote work.

For a work-heavy week, prioritize stable connectivity, electricity, quiet space, and services.

For a lighter week, remote areas and longer offline stretches may be fine.

In the mountains and during monsoon travel, plan for weather delays and connectivity gaps. Do not schedule a critical deployment immediately after a long transfer unless you have a tested fallback.

## 8. Build a compact but dependable travel kit

Start with what you own. Add gear only when it solves a real problem.

### Essential

- Laptop and compatible charger.
- Phone with a suitable data plan.
- Cables and adapters you actually use.
- Headphones for calls.
- Password manager and MFA recovery plan.
- Verified backups.
- A practical way to protect equipment in transit.

### Useful depending on the trip

- USB-C hub.
- Laptop-compatible power bank.
- Compact laptop stand.
- External SSD for backups.
- Travel router when it solves a specific network problem.
- Rain protection for gear on outdoor travel days.

Details live on [Gear & Tools](/gear)-linked honestly, no hype.

## 9. Your pre-trip checklist

<Checklist title="Before you leave">

- Confirm accommodation internet and workspace suitability.
- Check mobile coverage and prepare a backup connection.
- Update the laptop and test charger and cables.
- Pull repositories and queue offline-friendly tasks.
- Back up important files and verify recovery access.
- Check authentication, VPN, and remote-access requirements.
- Download tickets, maps, and essential travel details.
- Tell teammates or clients about your availability.
- Keep buffer time for delays and connectivity surprises.

</Checklist>

The best time to find gaps in your setup is **before** you leave-not when a deadline is due.

## 10. Keep the setup simple

Working while traveling gets easier when you remove unnecessary dependencies and know what you need to do your job.

A reliable laptop, a tested connection with a backup, a safe dev environment, and a realistic schedule beat an expensive pile of accessories.

Build around the work you actually do. Test before the trip, learn from problems, and refine as you travel. The goal is not to turn every destination into an office-it is to make work dependable enough that you can explore without constant worry.
